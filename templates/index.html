📖 Detailed Theory & Mathematical Derivation

1. Earth-Centered, Earth-Fixed (ECEF) Coordinate System

The ECEF system is a 3D Cartesian coordinate system ($X, Y, Z$) that rotates synchronously with the Earth. Its geometric center $(0,0,0)$ is fixed at the Earth's center of mass.

  • Z-axis: Points northward along Earth's true rotational axis (Conventional Terrestrial Pole).
  • X-axis: Lies in the equatorial plane, intersecting the Prime Meridian ($0^\circ$ longitude) at the equator.
  • Y-axis: Completes the right-handed orthogonal system, pointing towards $90^\circ$ East longitude at the equator.
ECEF 3D Cartesian Axes

2. WGS-84 Reference Ellipsoid

Because the Earth bulges at the equator due to centrifugal force, it is modeled as an oblate spheroid rather than a perfect sphere under the World Geodetic System 1984 (WGS-84) standard.

It is strictly defined by two geometric parameters: the semi-major equatorial radius ($a = 6,378,137\text{ m}$) and flattening ($f \approx 1 / 298.257$). Transforming ECEF Cartesian coordinates $(X, Y, Z)$ to geodetic coordinates maps the position onto this mathematical ellipsoid.

3. Mathematical Derivation of 3D Trilateration Equations

To find the unknown receiver position vector \((X, Y, Z)\) and receiver clock offset bias \(b\), we formulate simultaneous equations using signals from 4 visible satellites.

Step A: True Euclidean Distance (Geometric Distance)

$$d_i = \sqrt{(X - x_i)^2 + (Y - y_i)^2 + (Z - z_i)^2}$$

By Pythagorean theorem in 3D space, the true straight-line distance \(d_i\) between the receiver \((X, Y, Z)\) and satellite \(i\) located at known ECEF coordinates \((x_i, y_i, z_i)\).

Step B: Accounting for Receiver Clock Bias

$$P_i = C \cdot \Delta t_i = d_i + b$$

Radio signals travel at the speed of light \(C \approx 299,792,458 \text{ m/s}\). Multiplying the measured signal travel time \(\Delta t_i\) by \(C\) gives the pseudorange \(P_i\). It is called a "pseudo" range because consumer receiver clocks are not synchronized with atomic satellite clocks, introducing a clock error term \(b\) (expressed in meters of equivalent distance).

Step C: Assembling the 4-Satellite Equation System

$$\begin{aligned} P_1 &= \sqrt{(X - x_1)^2 + (Y - y_1)^2 + (Z - z_1)^2} + b \\ P_2 &= \sqrt{(X - x_2)^2 + (Y - y_2)^2 + (Z - z_2)^2} + b \\ P_3 &= \sqrt{(X - x_3)^2 + (Y - y_3)^2 + (Z - z_3)^2} + b \\ P_4 &= \sqrt{(X - x_4)^2 + (Y - y_4)^2 + (Z - z_4)^2} + b \end{aligned}$$

Because there are 4 unknown variables to solve for—three spatial position coordinates \((X, Y, Z)\) and one clock bias term \(b\)—exactly four independent satellite equations are required to find a unique, unambiguous solution.
